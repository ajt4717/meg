Question 1: “Paterson’s poem ‘The Man from Snowy River’ recaptures the indomitable spirit of the early Australians fighting for survival on a heroic scale.” Comment on this statement.

The emergence of a distinct, self-confident national literature in Australia in the late nineteenth century was intricately bound up with the rise of a robust national consciousness and the systematic dismantling of a derivative, Eurocentric colonial legacy. For over a century following the landing of the First Fleet in 1788, early Australian writing had been dominated by a profound sense of exile, as transplanted European writers viewed the alien, un-English landscape with a mixture of homesickness, surprise, and outright disgust. However, the launch of the influential journal The Bulletin in 1880 initiated a powerful movement for cultural nationalism. This journal provided an essential platform for native-born writers to reject old-world models, utilize the local vernacular, and celebrate the rugged, egalitarian values of the Australian outback.
At the absolute forefront of this literary revolution was Andrew Barton "Banjo" Paterson (1864–1941), who became the undisputed "chief balladist" and folk-poet of Australia. Published in 1895 as the title piece of his record-breaking first collection, “The Man from Snowy River” immediately captured the public imagination. The poem does not merely depict an exciting local chase; rather, it functions as a foundational national myth. It systematically elevates the daily, grueling struggles of early outback life to a grand, heroic scale, thereby immortalizing the "indomitable spirit" and resilient character of a newly emerging nation.
This representation of the early Australian spirit of survival on a heroic scale can be critically analyzed through the following thematic, structural, and historical points:
•	The Democratic Construction of the Bush Hero: The core thematic engine of "The Man from Snowy River" is its radical redefinition of the heroic figure, shifting the locus of valor away from traditional, aristocratic European models toward the ordinary, unpretentious commoner of the Australian outback. The narrative of the ballad centers on a call to gather "all the cracks" and "tried and noted riders" from stations "near and far" to recapture a valuable wild colt that has escaped and joined the mob of wild bush horses. Among the legendary, established figures of the region—such as the white-haired, wealthy Harrison, and the legendary Clancy of the Overflow—stands "a stripling on a small and weedy beast". The older figures of authority openly doubt the young lad's capacity to endure the journey, with Harrison patronizingly declaring that the weedy pony "will never do / For a long and tiring gallop". Yet, Clancy stands his friend, insisting that both the horse and the rider are "mountain bred" and therefore uniquely equipped for the trials ahead.
By showing that this slight, marginalized youth is the only rider brave enough to plunge down the sheer, terrifying mountain descent after the other horsemen have stopped short in "very fear," Paterson makes a powerful democratic point. The "stripling" represents the unfallen, resilient spirit of the new-born nation—a figure who, through sheer "pluck" and "hot" courage, successfully runs down the wild mob single-handed. This proves that personal worth in Australia is determined by competence, determination, and action, rather than pedigree or status.
•	The Symbol of the Horse and the Ethos of Horsemanship: To convey this indomitable spirit on an epic scale, Paterson utilizes the pivotal, active image of the horse and the accomplished horseman. In nineteenth-century Australia, the horse was of paramount physical and cultural importance, and the skilled stockman enjoyed unlimited, romantic hero-worship. Throughout the ballad, the word "horse" and its immediate associations ("colt," "saddle," "riding") are relentlessly repeated, establishing a pulsing, urgent rhythm of rapid movement and speed. The horses are not passive instruments of labor; they "snuff the battle with delight," and their hooves literally "strike firelight" from the flint stones with every stride.
The horseman and his beast function as a singular, symbiotic entity of physical energy and courage. The climax of the poem, wherein the Man from Snowy River lets his pony "have his head" and races down the mountain "like a torrent down its bed," is a magnificent, visual celebration of absolute physical vitality and "grace under pressure". By focusing on the extraordinary, death-defying feats of horsemanship, Paterson raises the daily work of the pastoral industry to the level of a classical, legendary chase, transforming the common stockman into a mythic figure comparable to Young Lochinvar, John Gilpin, or the Ancient Mariner.
•	The Confrontation with a Hostile and Untamed Landscape: The heroic scale of the struggle in the poem is further intensified by the imposing, naturalistic verticality and danger of the Australian landscape. Unlike the quiet, domestic, and heavily "poeticized" countryside of England, the Australian outback is presented as an immense, wild, and indifferent force that actively challenges the human presence. The Snowy River region, situated near Mount Kosciusko's side, is described as a rugged wilderness where the "hills are twice as steep and twice as rough," and the hidden ground is littered with treacherous "wombat holes, and any slip was death".
The physical setting functions as an obstacle course. The wild horses flee into "gorges deep and black" that resound to the "thunder of their tread," surrounded by "cliffs and crags that beetled overhead". The landscape is a severe, physical test designed to weed out the weak and the faint-hearted. When the riders reach the mountain's summit and the wild mob plunges down the vertical slope, the sheer danger "well might make the boldest hold their breath," causing the other horsemen to pull up in defeat. By setting the human action against this sublime, terrifying, and "torn" mountain terrain, Paterson successfully demonstrates how the harshness of the physical environment acts to mold and forge the "hard," "tough," and "wiry" survival instincts of the early pioneers.
•	The Philosophy of the Bush and the Spirit of "Giving it a Go": At its deepest philosophical level, "The Man from Snowy River" crystallizes the core values of the "Australian Legend"—the unwritten bush creed of stoicism, mateship, and a reckless, unyielding readiness to take risks. The indomitable spirit of the early Australians is characterized by what the cultural critic Clement Semmler describes as the ultimate national drive to "give it a go" and face down seemingly overwhelming odds. This philosophy is beautifully established through the mutual solidarity of the stockmen.
When the "old man" Harrison dismisses the young lad, it is Clancy who acts as the primary "mate," validating the youth's right to participate on the ground of his shared, mountain-bred heritage. When the chase begins, there is no room for "fancy riding" or self-preservation; it is a desperate, passionate commitment to action. The Man from Snowy River's refusal to surrender to fear, his willingness to let his horse "have his head" on a vertical descent where any slip meant death, is the ultimate enactment of this risk-taking ethos. Furthermore, his "undaunted" pluck is proven when he runs the mob "single-handed" until their sides are white with foam, following them "like a bloodhound" and bringing them back "alone and unassisted". This is not the tragic, doomed struggle of late-Victorian English verse; it is a robust, optimistic, and self-confident celebration of the common man's ability to conquer and master his environment through physical competence, endurance, and sheer, untamed will.
•	Aesthetic Innovations of the "Literary Ballad": While the themes of "The Man from Snowy River" are deeply rooted in the popular, unlettered folk-ballads and broadsides of the early convict and bush traditions, the poem’s immense, enduring success is the direct result of Paterson's highly sophisticated aesthetic craftsmanship. Rather than writing a rough, unpolished bush song, Paterson exercises tight "artistic control and verbal restraint" to elevate the form into a highly polished "literary ballad". The poem is structured with a sweeping, dactylic and anapestic rhythm that beautifully mimics the actual, physical movement of galloping horses, maintaining a high level of narrative excitement and dramatic suspense throughout.
While utilizing a robust, matter-of-fact colloquial style that "smacked of real-life conversation," Paterson meticulously avoids overly crude and "lurid" language. He utilizes the "language really spoken by people" but infuses it with a wealth of vivid, highly evocative imagery—such as the wild horses' sides "white with foam" and the "flint-stones flying"—which gives the dramatic action a clear, memorable, and cinematic quality. The poem’s final stanza shifts the tone from high, frantic action down to a quiet, mythic stasis. By describing how "Kosciusko's pine-clad ridges raise / Their torn and rugged battlements on high" in the "cold and frosty sky," Paterson grounds the legendary ride of the young hero in the timeless, sacred geography of the continent, ensuring that the story remains "a household word" to be told and retold by stockmen year after year.
Ultimately, "The Man from Snowy River" remains the undisputed classic of the Australian literary ballad because it beautifully transforms the raw, material hardships of the late-nineteenth-century pastoral outback into a permanent, highly prized monument of national self-definition. By celebrating the physical prowess, egalitarian camaraderie, and "indomitable" courage of the young stockman, Banjo Paterson provided his countrymen with a proud, heroic, and self-determining past to nourish their imagination as they transitioned into a modern, independent nation.

Question 2: “The image of the drover's wife subverts the stereotype of the woman as a helpless, clinging creature who needs to be protected by the powerful male.” Give your views on this statement on the basis of your reading of Henry Lawson’s story ‘The Drover’s Wife’.

The emergence of the realist short story in Australia during the late nineteenth century served as a vital vehicle to dismantle the heavily romanticized, male-centered myths of the Australian outback. Within this context, the concept of "mateship" arose primarily as an expression of male solidarity, vigor, and outdoor camaraderie, which deliberately excluded women, often delegating to them only the passive, silent virtues of stoicism and endurance. Henry Lawson’s masterly short story, "The Drover's Wife" (published in his landmark 1896 collection While the Billy Boils), stands as a radical, historically significant subversion of this patriarchal framework. Rather than portraying the bushwoman as a helpless, clinging creature who must depend entirely on the physical protection of her husband, Lawson places a gaunt, sun-browned, and completely independent female figure at the absolute center of his realistic narrative. Through a series of highly dramatic and finely detailed retrospective flashbacks, Lawson systematically deconstructs the romantic stereotype of female dependency, presenting the drover's wife as a powerful, autonomous, and self-reliant protector who battles against a hostile natural environment and social isolation with immense, stoic heroism.
The subversion of the female stereotype in the story can be analyzed in detail through the following points:
•	The Setting as a Site of Desolation and Monotony: The physical environment in which the drover's wife is situated serves to emphasize the stark, unromantic reality of her existence, directly contrasting with the grand, "saving illusions" of the pastoral outback celebrated by male balladists. Lawson describes her two-roomed home as built of round timber, slabs, and stringy-bark, with an earthen "ground floor" in the kitchen. The landscape is a flat, horizonless expanse of bush consisting of "stunted, rotten native apple-trees" with "no undergrowth" and "nothing to relieve the eye save the darker green of a few sheoaks".
This "everlasting, maddening sameness of the stunted trees" represents a suffocating monotony that makes men "long to break away and travel as far as trains can go, and sail as far as ships can sail". Yet, while men escape this maddening confinement by going on long droving trips, the drover's wife is "used to the loneliness of it". Her long-standing habituation to this isolation—having once lived like this for eighteen months all alone—redefines the bushwoman not as a victim who cowers in the absence of her husband, but as a resilient entity who has successfully adapted her entire psychological makeup to survive in a desert of social emptiness.
•	The Present Crisis of the Snake and the Maternal Protective Instinct: The immediate, dramatic action of the story is structured around the arrival of a deadly snake that slips under the floorboards of the house, forcing the drover's wife to undertake a tense, all-night vigil in her kitchen to protect her four children. Lawson immediately establishes her proactive, protective instincts. On hearing her son Tommy yell "Snake!", the gaunt bushwoman does not scream or faint; instead, she "dashes from the kitchen, snatches her baby from the ground, holds it on her left hip, and reaches for a stick". She puts her children to sleep on the large kitchen table, while she sits down beside them to watch with her dog, Alligator, until morning.
This situation becomes a quiet, domestic battlefield where the woman's maternal instinct is shown to be far more powerful than any masculine code of self-preservation. She is "on guard" not because she expects a husband to arrive and rescue her, but because she knows that she is the sole protector of her household. Her protective vigil, which is completed when the dog Alligator grabs the snake and the mother beats it to death with a stick, serves as a direct, physical demonstration of her autonomy and survival capacity.
•	The Retrospective Catalog of Conquered Crises: While she sits late into the night, the progression of her thoughts and flashbacks reveals that the snake is merely the latest in a long, grueling sequence of natural disasters that she has battled and conquered entirely on her own.
o	She recalls how she successfully fought a bushfire when her husband was away, putting on an old pair of his trousers to "beat out the flames with a green bough, till great drops of sooty perspiration stood out on her forehead".
o	She remembers fighting a flood, standing for hours in the drenching rain as she "dug an overflow gutter to save the dam across the creek".
o	She recalls fighting the pleuro-pneumonia that struck their cattle, dosing and bleeding the remaining stock, and confronting a mad bullock that besieged the house for a day, which she eventually shot through the cracks in the slabs with an old shotgun, skinning him in the morning to sell his hide for seventeen-and-sixpence.
o	She regularly fights the crows and eagles that threaten her chickens, using a broomstick like a gun and shouting "Bung!" to scare them away.
This long, detailed record of agricultural and domestic combat permanently shatters the Victorian stereotype of the delicate, helpless, and dependent "elegant female" [2.5.4]. The drover's wife is shown to have taken up every masculine role—the firefighter, the canal-digger, the cattle-doctor, the hunter, and the farmer—performing each task with extraordinary competence and stoic resolve.
•	The Confrontation with Male Intruders and Economic Autonomy: The drover's wife's self-reliance is further tested by the arrival of suspicious, hostile male outcasts who travel the bush tracks. She has had to develop specific, cunning strategies of self-defense to protect herself from "villainous-looking sundowners" and "bushmen in the horrors" who seek to take advantage of an unprotected woman. Her defense mechanism is based on the deliberate, defensive lie that "her husband and two sons are at work below the dam".
When a cringing, "gallows-faced swagman" realizes there are no men on the property and arrogantly demands food and shelter, she does not cower. Instead, she gets a batten from the sofa, unleashes her fierce dog Alligator, and confronts the stranger with absolute, unshaken determination: "Now you go!" she says, forcing the cringing man to pack his swag and flee. This physical and psychological triumph over a threatening male intruder proves that her survival is not dependent on male protection; indeed, she is more than capable of protecting herself and her territory against the very males who would seek to exploit her vulnerability. Furthermore, her economic reality is one of sheer, unadorned deprivation. She has no money of her own, since "women in the bush never have money," and her care-free, romantic girlish hopes and "castles in the air" have long been dead, showing the harsh, material cost of her survival.
•	The Rejection of the Outback Legend and the Birth of a New Loyalty: The emotional climax of the story is achieved through the young boy Tommy's reaction to his mother's suffering and exhaustless toil. After the snake is finally killed and thrown into the fire, the exhausted mother hugs her young son to her "worn-out breast". In this poignant moment of shared grief and exhaustion, the eleven-year-old Tommy looks at his mother's tearful, blackened face and fiercely declares: "Mother, I won't never go drovin' blarst me if I do!". This precocious, passionate declaration represents a radical, direct rejection of the celebrated outback legend and the masculine code of droving.
The boy realizes that the "glory" of his father's free, wandering life is built directly upon the slow, spiritual, and physical destruction of his mother left behind in the desolate, lonely hut. By choosing to stay and protect his mother rather than participating in the romanticized "droving" of his father, Tommy validates her suffering and inaugurates a new, realistic, and companionate code of domestic loyalty. Lawson's dry, realistic style—which completely avoids the sentimental cliches of the "sad Australian sunset" and the "grizzled head" of a weeping mate—proves that the drover's wife is the true, unheralded hero of the Australian settlement, whose unyielding strength has subverted the hollow pretenses of patriarchal protection.

Question 3: Critically examine how the interrelation between land and identity becomes a central theme in Patrick White’s novel Voss.

Patrick White’s masterpiece Voss (1957) stands as one of the most intellectually ambitious, structurally complex, and metaphysically profound achievements in modern Australian fiction. Written during a post-war era when Australian literature was struggling to break free from the limiting, "dun-coloured" conventions of journalistic realism, Voss transformed the historical and geographical landscape of the continent into a vibrant, mythic space.
Based on the actual, tragic nineteenth-century expeditions of the German explorer Ludwig Leichhardt, the novel systematically deconstructs the simple, materialistic relationship between the settlers and the land. Through the complex, spiritual journey of the central protagonist, Johann Ulrich Voss, White demonstrates that the physical land of Australia is not a passive material object to be bought, sold, and cleared; rather, it is an active, controlling, and terrifying presence that acts as a profound "objective correlative" for the human subconscious—creating a vast, inscrutable "country of the mind".
The profound interrelation between land and identity in Voss can be critically examined through the following points:
•	The Dialectical Contrast: Material Settlers vs. the Visionary Explorer: White structures his thematic inquiry around a sharp, ironic contrast between the middle-class, materialistic settlers of New South Wales and the visionary, metaphysical German explorer, Voss. The bourgeois settlers of Sydney, represented by the wealthy merchant Mr. Bonner, are fundamentally alienated from the land they have colonized. They view Australia purely in terms of material progress, property, and agricultural output. To them, the land is a blank space to be made secure, and their "understanding" of it is limited to the buildings they erect and the money they make. Their relationship with the outback is characterized by fear; they driving out for occasional picnics but are happy to return to the safe, conventional shelter of their suburban homes, viewing the vast interior of the continent as a barren, terrifying grave of "blacks, and deserts, and rocks, and skeletons".
In contrast, Voss rejects this comfortable, material security. For Voss, the land is a spiritual, metaphysical challenger. When Tom Radclyffe rudely asserts that the country does not belong to Voss because he is a foreigner, Laura Trevelyan counters with the central, defining thesis of the novel: "It is his by right of vision". Voss does not seek to make a fortune; he seeks to master the land through the absolute, egoistic "will to power," discovering in the vast, empty deserts of the continent the only space immense enough to match his own romantic delusions of divinity.
•	The Spatial Progression as an "Adventure Inwards": The geographical movement of Voss’s expedition down the continent is designed as a gradual, psychological transition from being to "becoming," moving away from the civilizing constraints of Europe toward the raw, primal truth of the self.
o	The journey initially begins in the gentility of New South Wales, before taking the party by sea to Newcastle.
o	They progress to Rhine Towers, the property of Mr. Sanderson, which represents a pastoral Eden of simplicity and peace. Here, the land is gentle and healing, and the settlers live in harmony with the soil.
o	From this sublime valley, the party moves to Jildra, the last outpost of the civilised world. Jildra represents a twilight zone of transition; set under "heavy moons" of bronze and gold, it is a space of "sleep-walking" and dreamlike hallucinations where the rational mind begins to disintegrate.
o	Beyond Jildra lies the desert—the flat, red rock, and quartz of the interior. Here, the landscape is stripped of all cultural landmarks, presenting a vast, silent "void of Nature" that forces the explorers to discard the "inessential" and confront the absolute "infinite" of their own souls.
•	The Telepathic Co-Extension of Voss and Laura: The interrelation between the land and human identity is further mystified by the extraordinary, telepathic relationship between Voss and Laura Trevelyan. Although Laura remains physically in Sydney while Voss travels into the desert, they remain indissolubly united in spirit. The vast, silent empty spaces of Australia, instead of separating them, act as a conductor for their thoughts. They share the same "hallucinatory" and "visionary" experiences, riding northward together in their dreams through "small hills, some green and soft... others hard and blue as sapphires".
Laura becomes Voss’s "conscience" and his companion in the desert, telling him: "You are my desert". When Voss is captured by the Aborigines and faces physical destruction in the wilderness, Laura, miles away in Sydney, is struck down by a mysterious, life-threatening "brain fever". This telepathic link demonstrates that their search for identity is a collaborative, shared experience; the physical desert of Voss’s expedition and the "country of the mind" in Laura’s fevered delirium are the same, proving that true, spiritual knowledge can only be achieved through suffering, failure, and the death of the ego.
•	The Aboriginal Presence and the Ritual of Self-Extinction: As the expedition penetrates deeper into the interior, their rational, European tools of science and navigation begin to fail them. The loss of their compass is highly symbolic, signifying their separation from the civilised, ordered world. In this wilderness, they encounter the Aborigines, who are presented not as social "savages," but as the natural, uninhibited human manifestation of the land itself. The Aborigines have "painted their bodies with the warm colours of the earth," existing in complete harmony with the soil totem by totem.
The tragic, violent division of the expedition—wherein the physically strong, practical Judd decides to turn back with Turner and others, while the visionary, "mad" Voss pushes forward to his death—exposes the "faultlines" within the white settler identity. Voss's eventual death, decapitated by the young Aborigine Jackie, is not a simple defeat; rather, it is a spiritual, ritualistic "sacrifice" through which his blood is poured upon the dry earth, which drinks it up immediately. By shedding his ego and dying in the desert, Voss finally conquers his pride and achieves the sublime, Christian status of "becoming" one with the land.
•	The Legend of the Land and the Ascent to History: The novel concludes with a return to the Sydney gentry years after the expedition. The people remain curious about the fate of Voss, and the returned, broken Judd offers a chaotic, contradictory report of his final days. But for Laura, the meaning of Voss's exploration is clear and complete. She asserts that Voss did not die, but has become an immortal, permanent part of the Australian landscape and history. He is there still in the country, and always will be, his "legend" having been written down by those who have been troubled by it.
Ultimately, Patrick White demonstrates that the identity of Australia cannot be built on the superficial, commercial conquests of the "rich men" and the "journalist rules" of Sydney; rather, it must be earned through the profound, spiritual sacrifice of those who, like Voss, are willing to suffer, fail, and die in the desert to find "the infinite". The interrelation of the land and identity is completed when the physical soil and the human spirit are twined together in the permanent, mythic consciousness of the nation.

Question 4: The poem “We are Going” by Kath Walker “depicts the murder of an entire civilization and way of life.” Give your response to this statement.

The publication of Kath Walker’s (later known as Oodgeroo Noonuccal) debut poetry collection, We Are Going (1964), was a landmark moment in Australian literary history. For nearly two centuries, the indigenous Aboriginal population of the continent had been completely marginalized, silenced, and pushed to the background by a dominant, "white" colonial culture. Traditional white histories and narratives had relied on the legal fiction of "terra nullius" (an empty land) to justify the violent conquest, dispossession, and systematic destruction of Aboriginal cultures. When Aborigines were depicted in early white literature, they were either dismissed as "half-human" and "dying" savages, or treated as "exotics" with whom one had nothing in common.
Kath Walker, writing as a proud, politically active member of her dispossessed people, used her poetry to shatter this silence. Her title poem, "We Are Going," stands as a devastatingly beautiful, quiet, and deeply poignant elegiac protest that documents "the murder of an entire civilization and way of life".
The depiction of this cultural and physical genocide in the poem can be analyzed through the following detailed points:
•	The Setting as an Arena of Desecration and Scorn: The opening lines of the poem immediately establish the agonizing contrast between the ancient, natural world of the Aborigines and the mechanical, crowded, and disrespectful world of the white invaders. The poem depicts a "semi-naked band" of Aborigines—"all that remained of their tribe"—coming into a little, modern town. They go to the place which was once their "old bora ground"—a sacred, hallowed site where young boys were initiated into the laws of manhood.
Instead of finding their sacred space, they are confronted by the frantic, mindless, and mechanical busyness of "many white men" who "hurry about like ants". The ultimate desecration of their culture is captured in the stark, cringing image of a notice of an estate agent that reads: "Rubbish May be Tipped Here". The rubbish of the white town now "half covers the traces of the old bora ring," symbolizing how the material waste and spiritual indifference of the dominant white society have systematically buried, choked, and erased the sacred monuments of their ancestors.
•	The Agony of Dispossession and Alienation: Faced with this desecration, the Aborigines are portrayed as "subdued and silent," sitting in a state of deep, painful confusion. The tragedy of their situation is that they are "strangers" in their own ancestral land. As the poetic voice declares: "We are as strangers here now, but the white tribe are the strangers. We belong here, we are of the old ways". The poem successfully subverts the white man's claim of ownership.
By asserting that the "white tribe" are the true, uninvited strangers, Walker highlights the historical criminality of the invasion. Yet, because they are politically powerless and have been "civilized" under duress, the Aborigines "cannot say their thoughts". Their silence is not the silence of consent; it is the silence of absolute, unwashed grief and the trauma of having their very "belonging" denied by the legal and economic structures of the colonizers.
•	The Catalog of the Vanishing Civilization: To convey the scale of the cultural murder, the poem develops a sweeping, litany-like catalog of all the elements of Aboriginal life that have been destroyed. The speakers declare their absolute, indissoluble identification with their traditional cultural practices:
o	"We are the corroboree and the bora ground," representing their ancient drama, music, and sacred initiation.
o	"We are the old sacred ceremonies, the laws of the elders," representing their complex, stable moral and legal structures.
o	"We are the wonder tales of Dream Time, the tribal legends told," representing their rich, complex oral literature and sacred history of creation.
o	"We are the past, the hunts and the laughing games, the wandering camp fires".
By using the present tense—"We are"—the speakers assert that they do not merely "practice" these traditions; they are these traditions. The systematic destruction of these rituals by the white administration and the church missions means that the very "self" of the Aboriginal people has been murdered, leaving them as "shadow-ghosts creeping back as the camp fires burn low".
•	The Parallel Extermination of Nature and Culture: One of the most profound, modern insights of the poem is its recognition that the "murder" of the Aboriginal civilization is inseparably linked to the destruction of the natural environment. Within the Aboriginal world-view, there was no division between "nature" and "culture"; they were part of one, integrated spiritual system of totemic kinship. The speakers declare: "We are the lightning-bolt over Gaphembah Hill... and the Thunder... We are the quiet daybreak paling the dark lagoon".
When, therefore, the white man "rapes" and "clears" the land to establish his towns, he kills both the landscape and the people. The final lines of the poem are a tragic, cumulative, and elegiac dirge for this dual extinction:
o	"The scrubs are gone, the hunting and the laughter".
o	"The eagle is gone, the emu and the kangaroo are gone from this place".
o	"The bora ring is gone. The corroboree is gone".
o	"And we are going."
The physical disappearance of the native animals and the destruction of the scrubs are the direct precursors to the final, imminent disappearance of the Aboriginal people themselves. The land has been denuded, made barren and "empty" by the despoilers, and the civilization that lived in harmony with it for forty thousand years is going into the dark.
Ultimately, Kath Walker's "We Are Going" is a masterpiece of public, political, and elegiac art. It does not rely on a loud, screaming rage; indeed, its power derives from its quiet, solemn, and unyielding truthfulness. By showing that the destruction of the Aborigines and the clearing of the land are two faces of the same, violent colonial crime, the poem forces the reader to confront the "dark side of the dream" of Australian civilization, proving that the birth of the white nation has been purchased with the murder of an ancient, sacred, and irreplaceable way of life.

Question 5: How does Malouf re-imagine Australian colonial history in Remembering Babylon?

David Malouf’s seventh novel, Remembering Babylon (1993), stands as one of the most brilliant, lyrically sophisticated, and historically profound "revisionist" texts in contemporary post-colonial fiction. Set on the north Queensland frontier in the mid-nineteenth century, the novel was inspired by the historical records of the shipwrecked English sailor, James Morrill, who lived for seventeen years among the aborigines before being "restored" to white society.
Rather than writing a simple, documentary historical chronicle, Malouf uses this historical event as a fluid, poetic, and metaphysical metaphor. He systematically re-imagines the colonial past to expose the deep-seated anxieties, "faultlines," and racial paranoia that constructed the white Australian identity, while simultaneously proposing a new, alternative path of ecological harmony and spiritual reconciliation with the first Australians.
Malouf’s re-imagining of colonial history in the novel can be critically examined through the following detailed points:
•	The Figure of Gemmy and the Deconstruction of the Colonial Boundary: Malouf structures his historical re-imagining around the arrival of Gemmy Fairley, a white English youth who, after being cast away, lived for sixteen years with an Aboriginal tribe. Gemmy is a "white black-man"—a human palimpsest whose original, civilized identity has been overlaid and almost effaced by his experience of living with the aborigines. When Gemmy suddenly emerges from the ti-tree swamp and balances on the boundary fence of the small farming settlement, his first words are: "Do not shoot, I am a B-b British object!". This dramatic, tragi-comic entrance is highly symbolic.
By standing on the "fence" that divides the raw, cleared white settlement from the vast, unknown "Absolute Dark" of the swamp, Gemmy's very presence deconstructs the rigid, colonial boundaries between "savagery" and "civilization," black and white, the "self" and the "other". He is a "between-world's" character who challenges the white settlers' sense of security. His existence forces them to confront the terrifying possibility that their own civilized identity is a fragile, temporary veneer that could easily be eroded by the hostile country they are striving to tame.
•	The Paranoia of the Settlers and the "Absolute Dark" of the Land: Malouf’s historical re-imagining exposes the psychological "autism" and deep-seated panic of the early pioneers. The settlers—mostly working-class refugees from the poverty and "uncleanliness" of the British Industrial Revolution—are psychologically adrift in Australia. Having left behind an old world of "hedges between fields that went back a thousand years" and "names on headstones," they find themselves in an immense, unploughed, and un-home-like landscape that they have no means of decoding.
Their response to this territory is aggressively destructive. They set about "stripping" the land of every vestige of the native, "ring-barking" and clearing the forest in a frantic, futile effort to make the land "just a wee bit like home". They live in constant fear of being "overwhelmed" by the "black Myall" natives who roam without acknowledging their boundaries. By showing how a simple, quiet meeting between Gemmy and two Aborigines in a paddock triggers off a "bushfire" of collective, violent paranoia and dacoits among the settlers (led by Ned Corcoran and Andy McKillop), Malouf realistically exposes the criminality of the "white Australia" mind.
•	The Conflict of Wills: The Colonial Machine vs. the Promised Land: The novel contrasts several different, competing strategies of interacting with the land. On the one hand is the rigid, bureaucratic, and imperialistic "will to power" represented by the state officials. The Governor of Queensland, Sir George Bowen, is a pompous, "monstrously ambitious" civil servant who views Australia as a blank page on which he will "create spaces where history may now occur," while the Premier, Mr. Herbert, treats the colony merely as a Horatian, aristocratic retreat from "the rough and tumble of colonial democracy".
On the other hand is the alternative, "androgynous" path of reconciliation represented by Mr. Frazer, the botanizing minister, and Jock McIvor. Mr. Frazer, guided by Gemmy, realizes that the settlers are starving in the midst of plenty because they look at the country with "English eyes" that only see "what is known and expected". He argues in his journal that if they learn to accept the land as it is, Australia will become their "Promised Land" rather than a place of exile. Similarly, Jock McIvor, after being isolated by his former friends for protecting Gemmy, undergoes a profound, "sensory" epiphany while walking in a field. By observing the beauty of "hundreds of tiny iridescent insects" and the "western sky drenched in flame," Jock is cured of his homesickness and finds a peaceful, private reconciliation with the Australian earth.
•	The Paradox of Language and the Written Word: The theme of language and communication is central to Malouf's historical re-imagining. Language in Remembering Babylon is shown to be a double-edged sword that has both creative and destructive powers. When Gemmy first enters the settlement, he has lost his English and must rely on gestures, signs, and the sensory communication of his body to interact with the settlers. However, the young schoolmaster George Abbot and the minister Frazer try to "capture" Gemmy's life by writing it down on seven sheets of paper.
This written "history" is seen by Gemmy as a dangerous, "black magic" that has drained his spirit from him and trapped his "life" on a page. He is obsessed with stealing these papers back. This is a powerful, meta-fictional critique of history writing itself, suggesting that the written, "official" history of the colonizers is an artificial construct that "imprisons" the true, fluid, and living reality of the land and its people. Ultimately, Gemmy retrieves the paper—which turns out to be only the wet, soggy exercises of schoolboys—and drops it into puddles in the rain, letting his written "history" dissolve back into the Australian earth.
•	The Epiphanic Climax: Fast-Forwarding to Reconciliation: The final chapter of the novel represents a dramatic shift in both time and space, fast-forwarding fifty years to the eve of the First World War. The narrative brings together Janet McIvor (now Sister Monica, a nun) and her cousin Lachlan Beattie (now a Cabinet Minister). As they reflect on their childhood, they acknowledge their love and their shared burden of guilt over the violent "dispersal" and murder of the Aborigines in which Gemmy was killed.
The novel concludes with Janet's magnificent, "sensory" vision of the rising tide under the moonlight. As the water approaches the shore, the vast, dark continent of Australia is seen to be "in touch now with its other life"—a life that includes both the white history of settlement and the ancient, spiritual Dreaming of the Aborigines.
Through this beautiful, poetic, and cathartic ending, David Malouf successfully re-imagines Australian colonial history. He suggests that the true identity of the nation can only be realized when the "white" and "black" histories are re-membered, and the people learn to approach the land and one another with the quiet humility of "prayer" and "knowledge".
